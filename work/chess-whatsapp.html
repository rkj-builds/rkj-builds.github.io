How Chess on WhatsApp works

What happens between sending PLAYCHESS and seeing a board: the route a message takes, the messages WhatsApp actually receives, the rules they keep, how the coach decides what to say, and what the demo can never do.

What to expect

  1. A hello, in two languages. First a line saying this is a concept demo. Then the club's own hello in your country's language and English. From India that's Hindi; from Brazil, Portuguese; from elsewhere, English and Hindi. Three buttons: those two languages, and "Other languages", a list of the ten others.
  2. Today's puzzle. The board as a picture, whose move it is, how many tries you have, and three moves to tap. Typing a move works too, as Qe3 or e2e4.
  3. The coach. A wrong move gets a hint that names the piece, then the square. After three tries it shows the answer. A right move gets a reply, then the opponent's answer as a new board.
  4. The end. A link to the puzzle on Chess.com, and buttons for another puzzle or the other language.

It's a demo, so it forgets you after 30 minutes without a message. Send PLAYCHESS again to start over, or "stop" to end it at any time. Nothing you send is kept by the venue's systems.

The route a message takes

The number belongs to Project 831, the sports venue I co-founded. Its bot already takes real bookings and payments on WhatsApp, so the chess club runs beside it, never inside it.

  1. You send PLAYCHESS. WhatsApp delivers it to Meta's Cloud API.
  2. Meta posts it to 831's bot, as it does with every message to the number.
  3. The demo router claims it before anything else runs. The keyword, a tap on a demo button, or a chat already in a demo goes to the demo. Nothing is logged to the inbox, saved, or sent to the team. The router forwards the message to the chess club service, signed with a shared secret, with a hash in place of your number.
  4. The chess club answers with the messages to send: the hello, a puzzle, the coach's words. It fetches today's puzzle from Chess.com's public API and draws the board as a picture.
  5. The bot checks the answer and sends it from the same number, to you only.
  6. WhatsApp shows it. To draw the board, Meta fetches the picture from the chess club.

A business can't start a chat without an approved templateOn this number you write first, so no template is needed. In the full concept, the morning puzzle is a template.

The checks run on every message before it leaves, and in tests for every language. A message WhatsApp would refuse is never sent.

How the coach decides what to say

The coach is an agent run in three steps, and the model is the last of them.

  1. Read the move. The server plays it on the board with chess.js: right, wrong, not legal here, or not a move at all. The model never judges the move.
  2. Choose what to give away. The first wrong try names the piece. The second names the square. After the third, the answer. The model is only told what this step allows, so it can't give more away by accident.
  3. Write it. Claude writes the words in your language, in a sentence or two, with the words players say at a board. A reply already written for the same moment comes from a 7-day cache. Without a model, capped or failed, the engine's prepared lines answer instead.

Live coaching is capped at 40 sessions a day, 3 per person and 8 replies a session. On the WhatsApp number today, the coach answers with the engine's lines: the service has no model key yet.

What the demo can never do

  • Touch the venue. No booking, payment, inbox message or team alert. A test walks the whole chess demo with the database, payments, templates and alerts replaced by traps, and fails if any of them is touched.
  • Learn your number. The chess club gets a salted hash and the first three digits, for the country. It never sees your number or a WhatsApp token.
  • Message anyone else. The bot addresses every reply to the person who wrote. The club can't name a recipient.
  • Ask for money. Only text, reply buttons and lists pass through. Payment requests, forms and anything else are dropped.
  • Be called by someone else. The club only answers a body signed with the shared secret, and refuses one more than five minutes old.
  • Stay on against my wishes. One setting on the bot switches every demo off, from its next deploy.

Where the code lives for engineers

  • The chess club: TypeScript, chess.js and Claude, as two functions on Vercel. One answers the bot; one draws boards with resvg and a cut-down DejaVu font. 83 tests.
  • The router and the forwarder: Node in Project 831's bot. A registry of demos, each with a keyword, start, tap and typed handlers, plus stop words, a 30-minute session and dedupe, all in memory.
  • Both repositories are private. I'm glad to walk through either.
