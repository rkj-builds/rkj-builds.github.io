01 Where the money was leaking

Extras were an interruption between a decision and a form.

Le Boat sells week-long canal holidays. Extras aren't upsells bolted onto a cart — damage cover, fuel, early check-in and bike hire are the things that decide what the week actually feels like. The old checkout treated them as an aside.

The flow put contact details first, then extras, then payment. By the time a traveller reached the extras, they had already filled in a form, already shifted out of planning mode and into transaction mode. Extras sat behind accordions, priced but not explained, and the summary that should have anchored the decision was a passive block you couldn't open, edit or act on.

We catalogued six failures in the existing experience:

  • Disrupted decision-making — the form broke the planning mindset before extras were even shown.
  • Minimal value communication — a name and a price, with no account of what the money buys.
  • High decision effort — travellers had to interpret value, evaluate risk and compare options unaided.
  • No information hierarchy — a flat list where a £2,970 liability sat level with a £50 gift basket.
  • Limited recommendations or guidance — nothing signalled what mattered or what people like them chose.
  • A passive summary — extras detail hidden, no way to edit or remove.

What the session recordings showed

Behaviour told the same story before anyone was interviewed. Four patterns recurred, and each one reads as a specific kind of friction rather than general dissatisfaction:

Observed behaviourWhat it indicates
Users trying to navigate backUnclear where they were in the flow
Rage-clicking the summaryExpected an interaction that wasn't there
Zig-zag scrollingPoor structure — no scannable order
Repeatedly switching between optionsDecision uncertainty, not comparison

Rage-clicking a summary is the most useful of these. It isn't a usability nit — it's a traveller telling you, with their mouse, that the running total is the object they want to manipulate. That single observation ended up driving more of the redesign than any stated preference did.

The conclusion we took into design: users weren't skipping extras because they didn't want them. They were skipping because the interface gave them no way to decide confidently.

About the numbers in this case study

Every research figure, participant quote and build detail here is drawn from the actual study and the actual repository — the 36-participant usability test, the twelve findings, the commit history, the accessibility audit. The commercial figures are now real too. The flow went live on 20 July 2026 and §10 reports what the analytics actually show, including the result that contradicts the flattering version of this story. Where a number is still a model rather than a measurement — the opportunity sizing in §4, the experiment maths in §9 — it says so on the face of it, with its assumptions exposed and adjustable.

The caveats, stated up front

The new prototype was always shown first, so time-on-task and engagement figures carry an order effect, and the current flow was always reviewed second, making its feedback comparative rather than standalone. The prototype also ran with lower latency than a production build would. I treated preference (69%) as a strong signal and every engagement metric as directional only — which is exactly why the flow shipped with instrumentation and a live A/B plan rather than a claimed win.

03 Getting answers out of the organisation

Most of what a decision needs isn't on the screen.

It's a number somebody else owns. The work is knowing which question to ask, who holds the answer, and what to build when nobody does.

Three kinds of question, going to three different places. What does the system actually do — engineering. What does the business already know — whoever owns the funnel. What does the customer actually do — the recordings and the research. A question answered late becomes a rebuild. The same question answered early is a line in a spec.

The question, and who held the answerWhat came back, and what it changed
"What actually happens between step 2 and step 3?"
Engineering		 A backend call re-prices the booking with the chosen extras, and it takes 3–5 seconds. Nobody had designed for that window. Left alone it's a button that looks broken at the exact moment a traveller has committed — so it became an explicit waiting state with the delay named in copy.
"What's the real shape of the booking-note payload?"
Engineering		 An unbounded array of strings from the reservation system; production surfaced only the first. That answer became a business contract — at most 3 notes, 400 characters each — enforced in a tested function rather than hoped for upstream.
"Does this read as our site, or as a redesign of it?"
Brand		 It didn't — and the answer came back as specific values from the live site rather than as an opinion: the primary and the canvas were wrong, and there was "too much blue and navy". Colour arguments end fastest when the other party can hand you a number.
"Open the promo field by default, or collapse it?"
The business		 Open — and it came back already A/B tested. The most valuable answer in the project was one I didn't have to earn. Ask what has already been tested before proposing a test.
"What's the exact deposit split, and who signs it off?"
Commercial		 Unresolved — and still unresolved at handover. So the rate became a constant and the schedule a pure function of it: the number can move without a redesign, a retest or another meeting. An open question is a parameter, not a blocker.
"Is this comparison inverted?"
Engineering, unprompted		 A hash check in the production payment utility looked backwards, and it gates payment validation. Reading somebody else's code and asking one question protects more revenue than most design decisions do.

The standing questions

These go into the first conversation of any growth engagement, before anything is designed. They're ordered so that a vague answer to one makes the next one sharper.

  1. What is the one number this work should improve? If two people answer differently, that is the finding, and it's a bigger problem than anything on the page.
  2. What do we already know and have never acted on? Most organisations are sitting on a finished study. Acting on it costs nothing and buys the credibility to run the tests that follow.
  3. Where does that number come from, and who else reads it? A metric with two definitions in two dashboards can't settle an argument.
  4. What's the smallest change you'd ship to everyone if it won? The real scope question. It exposes appetite before effort is spent.
  5. What would make you stop the rollout? Agreeing guardrails while everyone is calm is the only time it's easy.
  6. What can't change, and why? Contractual, regulatory, operational. Cheap to ask now; expensive to discover in review.
The pattern underneath all six

Each one converts a design conversation into a question with a falsifiable answer. That's the actual craft; the interface is downstream of it. A decision that can't be traced to a number somebody owns is a preference, and preferences don't survive a quarter.

Where those questions stand

Recording which ones are still open is part of the method, not an admission. An unanswered question you can name is a research task; an unanswered question you've quietly replaced with an assumption is a liability you'll meet again at the read-out.

04 Sizing the prize before drawing anything

What is a point of attach rate worth?

Before the first screen, the question that decides whether this work is worth a sprint: what does moving extras attach actually pay, and is it bigger than the risk of disturbing a working checkout?

Extras at Le Boat are high-margin and largely fixed-cost — damage cover, flex plans and prepaid fuel don't scale in cost with uptake the way a boat does. That makes attach rate one of the few levers where a design change flows close to directly to contribution. A deliberately simple model: incremental attached bookings × average extras basket. It ignores cannibalisation, refunds and seasonality. Attach rate stays a slider because, as §10 explains, the share of bookings containing an extra still cannot be measured in the analytics as configured.

At the observed volumes, seven points of attach rate is worth about £120,000 a year. That reframed the conversation with the business: the risk wasn't in changing the checkout, it was in leaving a structurally poor extras step in place for another season. It also set the bar for the experiment — and, as it turned out, the bar was the problem. At roughly 340 bookings a month, an effect of this size is very close to undetectable without a control group. That was knowable in advance. It is the whole argument of §9, and §10 is what happens when you ship without acting on it.

05 The structural move

Take the form out of the middle of the decision.

One change preceded every other: contact details stopped interrupting the part of the journey where people are still choosing what their week looks like.

Before

Trip → 1. Contact · 2. Extras · 3. Pay → Payment

A form gate before any extras were seen. Three numbered steps inside one page, so "where am I" had no honest answer. Extras collapsed behind accordions below the fold.

After

1. Your trip → 2. Complete your trip → 3. Review and pay

Extras become their own step, framed as completing the holiday. Contact details move next to payment, where the mental mode has already shifted from choosing to buying.

This restructure came out of the strategy work, not from me — it was proposed, prototyped and validated in testing before I started building. I'm including it because everything downstream depends on it, and because the rename mattered as much as the reorder. "Choose extras" became "Complete your trip." Under the old label, every card was a request for more money against a price the traveller had already accepted — which is precisely the price-shock finding (04). Under the new one, the same cards are the remaining decisions in planning a holiday. Nothing about the commercial content changed; the frame it arrives in did.

Five design principles came with it. I treated them as acceptance criteria rather than as a mood board, which is the difference between a principle and a poster:

Guide
Recommend what matters, rather than presenting a neutral list and calling it choice.
Simplify
Reduce decision effort. Fewer things visible at once, grouped by what they do for you.
Reassure
Build confidence and reduce perceived risk — with specifics, not adjectives.
Show value
Lead with the outcome, put the price second.
Support control
Never trap. Everything addable is removable, in one action, from where it's shown.

The button language changed on the same logic. Pay £1,559 asks a traveller to commit before they've reviewed anything, and people subconsciously read it as "am I being charged now?". The continue action became "Continue to review & pay" — it names the next screen, not the transaction, and it tells the truth about where the money actually moves.

06 Five decisions, and what each one buys

From finding to interface.

Each of these traces a straight line from a specific research finding to a specific piece of shipped front-end.

What I inherited, and what I decided

The exposed-card concept, the four outcome-led group names, the value-first copy and the flow order came out of the strategy work and the tested prototype. They were validated before I touched them, and claiming them would be dishonest. My work was everything between a validated concept and a production checkout — the decisions a concept prototype never has to make. Each decision below carries its provenance, and the screenshots are labelled Tested prototype Shipped build or Parked exploration so you can see which is which.

Decision 01 · answers findings 01 & 11

Extras get exposed as cards, and never go back behind a click.

The clearest result in the study. Users engage with what they can see; accordions hid the entire catalogue behind an interaction nobody had a reason to perform. The card format also did something the study didn't set out to measure — it changed how the checkout felt.

It feels like you're already going on holiday. P24, desktop — on the new prototype
It feels more aligned with insurance than booking an exciting trip. P6, mobile — on the current flow

Those two quotes describe the same catalogue at the same prices. The delta is entirely presentational, and it's the strongest argument in the study for treating extras as part of the product rather than as an ancillary revenue surface.

Decision 02 · answers findings 02 & 07 · mine

Grouping wasn't the problem. Nothing bounded the page.

The tested prototype already grouped extras under the four outcome-led headings, and testing confirmed people liked them. It still produced the study's second-biggest complaint: twelve participants reported scroll fatigue. Grouping labelled the page; it didn't shorten it. Every extra in every group still rendered, so the page got longer with every product the catalogue gained.

The study recommended collapsible categories. What shipped is progressive disclosure inside groups that stay open: each shows its top two cards, with a "Show N more" toggle for the remainder. Collapsing whole categories would have re-created the accordion problem that finding 01 had just told us to avoid — hiding extras behind a click people have no reason to perform. Two cards per group keeps every category visible while giving the page a fixed floor length no matter how big the catalogue gets.

¹ Action item #7 from the 6 July alignment meeting — "show top 2 per group, rest behind a Show N more toggle". The tested prototype has no such control; grep it for "show more" and you get nothing. commit 6bb5c86 · the checkout's extras catalogue
Decision 03 · answers finding 12 · inherited, then held

Keep the real numbers, including the untidy ones.

The card headline — "Avoid paying a damage deposit of up to £2,970" — came from the strategy work and was in the tested prototype. It is the single best piece of copy in the project: the price is anchored against the liability, not the fee, because £105 read next to £2,970 is a different proposition than £105 read alone. Finding 12 said the card still wasn't enough — three German participants wanted to know what deposit they'd actually pay.

The pressure at that point is to invent a clean answer. The study itself suggested the shape of one: "Your deposit drops from £2,970 to £500." Deposits at Le Boat don't work that way — they vary by boat size and destination. So the detail view carries the real ranges: £220–£660 with cover against £2,060–£2,970 without, plus the non-refundable condition in the same view.

A range is a worse headline and a better answer. It is also the only version that survives contact with a traveller who reaches the base and is quoted £640.

² Rendered as paired cards — "With Damage Cover" against "Without Damage Cover" — each carrying its deposit range and liability terms. the damage-cover pop-up's content
Decision 04 · answers findings 06 & 09

The summary becomes the thing you steer with.

This is the rage-click, answered. People were clicking the summary because they wanted it to respond. So it now does: it sticks to the viewport on desktop, updates the total as extras are added or removed, opens each extra for editing, and carries the trip context that finding 09 said people wanted before the extras rather than beneath them.

Having the summary always visible on the right is very important when about to click pay. P16, French, desktop

On mobile the same object becomes a fixed bottom bar with the running total and the continue action, expanding into a full booking summary sheet. Both bars were then made consistent with each other, because two sticky elements behaving differently on the two steps is a bug in the mental model even when neither is a bug in the code.

It's also the surface where the price-shock finding (04) gets managed honestly. The total moves in real time as extras go in, so the number at the payment step is never a surprise — the traveller watched it get there.

Decision 05 · answers finding 04 · mine

Give the price a second answer: pay a quarter now.

Finding 04 — the discount "swallowed up by extras" — has two possible responses. You can reduce what's being asked for, or you can change what's due today. The tested prototype did neither: its payment step offered card or wallet, an inline card form, and a Pay £1,169 button. That is precisely the premature-commitment language the strategy work flagged, and it leaves a price-sensitive traveller with one option — pay all of it, now.

The important part is that the business already had this lever. Production carries deposit, middle-payment and pay-in-full amounts in its booking payload — the capability exists and has always existed. The prototype simply didn't surface it, which would have shipped a redesign that quietly removed a conversion route the company already owned. Catching a regression like that is worth more than most features, and it is only catchable by reading the production system rather than the comp.

So what shipped restores it and makes it legible: 25% today to secure the boat, the balance in two interest-free instalments, with the trade-off stated rather than buried — pay in full and save 3%, or pay the deposit and keep the cash. Selecting the deposit expands the schedule with real dates and amounts, so "instalments" is never an abstraction. The CTA becomes "Pay £292 deposit", so the amount actually leaving the account today is never ambiguous.

(Code excerpt removed for sharing. What it does: the deposit is derived from the live total, so extras and discounts flow through, and the remainder lands on the final payment, property-tested so the parts always sum to the whole, at every total.)

I deliberately dropped the "Recommended" tag from the deposit option late in the build. Recommending a payment method isn't guidance — the better option depends entirely on the traveller's cash position, which we don't know. Labelling one as recommended would have been the design system pretending to information it didn't have.

07 The tension I didn't design away

The nudge that works on most people and offends some.

When a traveller continues without damage cover, a comparison appears showing what they're carrying: the deposit with cover against the deposit without. Finding 05 says this works. P4 called it "a big warning about how much I would have to pay" and meant it as a compliment. P10: "it was a good prompt."

Finding 05 also says French participant P17 found it "pushy" and felt it gave "the impression they're forcing you to subscribe". Both reactions are correct responses to the same component.

I kept the intervention and constrained it:

  • It states facts, not urgency. Two deposit ranges and what each means. No countdown, no scarcity, no "X people bought this in the last hour" on the protection products.
  • Declining costs one action. The traveller continues from the same view they're interrupted in. A nudge that takes two clicks to escape is a toll, not a prompt.
  • It fires once. Not on every continue, not again at payment.
  • The add action sits inside the comparison. If the number changes someone's mind, they act where they were convinced — not by hunting back up the page.

The honest position: this is the component most likely to be wrong, and it's first on the test roadmap. The study's own recommendation was to soften it further — an inline comparison rather than a blocking modal — and that variant is specified and unbuilt. It's an experiment, not a conviction.

Finding 10 is the related one I chose not to act on. Experienced travellers decline checkout insurance on principle and source it themselves. That's a rational, well-informed behaviour. Designing harder against it would mean designing against the customer's own interest, and the only tactics that move that segment are the ones this brand has explicitly ruled out.

08 Build once, ship twice

The cost of the second brand is the number that matters.

Le Boat and Emerald Star are two brands on one checkout. How much a validated win costs to ship to the second one decides how often anyone bothers — and that is an architecture decision, not a visual one.

The default outcome is a fork. Someone copies the checkout, re-skins it, and from that day the two diverge: every experiment gets designed twice, QA'd twice, made accessible twice, and eventually one of them stops getting the improvements. The second brand quietly becomes the worse product, and nobody decides that — it just accretes.

So every brand value is a semantic token with a single override block, and switching brand is one attribute on the document root. The consequence is commercial, not aesthetic: a change validated on Le Boat ships to Emerald Star the same day, at no additional design, build, QA or accessibility cost. That halves the price of every future experiment and doubles what each win is worth. Six locales across two brands is twelve live surfaces from one component tree.

Accessibility is reach, and reach is the denominator

A checkout that fails contrast doesn't fail a standard — it fails a share of the people already trying to give you money, at the last step, where there is no second chance. Every conversion rate in this case study has a denominator, and inaccessible interface silently removes people from it.

So the flow was swept to WCAG 2.1 AA with axe, both brands, desktop and mobile, closing at zero contrast violations — the unreadable greys, a duplicate landmark, an unlabelled sticky region, two touch targets under the minimum, and a primary colour doing two jobs that needed two values. It is the least glamorous work in the project and the only work that changes who is able to complete the purchase at all.

Where naive theming breaks, and what it costs

Most theming survives a logo swap and fails on the first real brand. It fails for one reason: the tokens encode colours rather than roles, so the moment the second brand's palette collides with a semantic meaning, someone forks a component — and the fork is where the cost comes back.

(Token excerpt removed for sharing: each brand sets its own values for the same brand roles.)

--brand-added is exactly that collision, caught before it forked anything. Confirming that an extra was added is green on Le Boat, where green reads as success against a blue brand. Emerald Star's brand is green — so the same token dissolves into the page and the confirmation stops confirming. A traveller who can't tell whether the extra went in either adds it twice or abandons the step, and that is an attach-rate problem wearing a colour costume.

The token was therefore defined by its job — contrast against whatever the brand is — and takes opposite hues in the two brands. One line of config instead of a second component, and the difference between the two compounds across every release for as long as both brands exist.

The same leverage shows up in the accessibility work. The WCAG 2.1 AA pass ran against both brands at once and closed at zero contrast violations on desktop and mobile. Forked, that's two audits forever — and in practice the second one gets skipped, which is how a co-brand ends up carrying the compliance risk nobody has budgeted for.

The unglamorous half

Fifteen error states were catalogued from the production checkout and mapped to the new flow before any of them were built — sold-out extras, partial availability, promo failures, declined payments, expired sessions — each with its surfacing pattern and its production copy key, reused verbatim so the work lifts back without a translation pass.

The business logic that had been living inline got pulled into pure, tested functions: the deposit schedule (with a property test proving the instalments always sum to the total, no rounding leak) and the booking-note normaliser (at most three notes, 400 characters each, truncated on a word boundary — eight cases). Those are the two places where a silent failure would be a financial or legal problem rather than a visual one.

What I parked, and said so

An extras discovery layer — search, filter chips, recommendations — was built, liked, and moved out of scope. It lives on its own branch with a written note recording where it is and how to restore it. Bundle-first variants were explored and not shipped; the study's evidence for bundles was thinner than for the card format, and 40% of travellers build their own. Shipping the structural win first and testing bundles separately keeps the result readable.

09 Instrumentation and proof

A preference signal is not a result.

69% of participants preferred the new flow. That is a reason to build it and not evidence that it converts. Here's the plan that was handed over with the code.

The metric set

One primary metric, chosen before the test runs. Secondaries to explain the mechanism. Guardrails that can stop a rollout even if the primary wins — because an extras uplift bought with a drop in booking completion is a loss wearing a win's clothes.

  • Scroll depth on the extras step — the direct measure of scroll fatigue.
  • Time to first extras interaction — how long orientation actually costs.

Writing that list is design work, not analytics housekeeping. A flow that ships without it produces a result nobody can explain — only observe. If the nudge wins, I want to know whether it won because it persuaded people or because it slowed them down enough to think.

Testing when you don't have the traffic

Le Boat is a considered, seasonal purchase. Checkout volume doesn't support the test cadence a high-frequency e-commerce site takes for granted, which changes the strategy rather than excusing it: test whole experiences, not components. A structural change produces an effect large enough to detect; a button-colour test on this traffic is a way to spend six weeks learning nothing.

So the flow shipped as one variant against the current checkout, and the calculator below is the one I used to argue for that scope — and to set expectations about how long an answer takes.

What runs after the first read-out

  1. Nudge treatment — blocking comparison modal against the study's recommended inline comparison. The ethics question, decided with data.
  2. Bundles — value-led bundle cards above build-your-own, against the flat catalogue. Held back so it doesn't confound the structural test.
  3. Category defaults — two cards visible against three, informed by the expand rate the first test produces.
  4. Discovery layer — the parked search-and-filter work, if category expand rates say the catalogue is outgrowing four groups.
10 The read-out

It shipped to eight locales. Here is what moved.

The redesign went live on 20 July 2026 — the redesign's feature flag flipped to true for every locale, reaching production the same morning. Ten weeks of data later, every metric the work targeted moved in the right direction.

The go-live is not an estimate, and the data confirms it without being told: the legacy flow's signature event — an accordion opening — falls 88% across that boundary. The accordion does not exist in the redesign.

Extras engagement nearly doubled

In the 71 days after launch against the 71 before, sessions adding at least one extra went from 14.75% to 27.13% of booking-stage sessions — a 12.4 point rise, +84% relative, on precisely the metric the restructure was built to move.

Travellers who buy extras now attach 4.31 per booking, up from 3.41 — 26% more separate decisions made on that step. And the guardrail held: progression to payment did not fall (5.62% → 5.86%). The step got busier without getting slower.

The market these numbers were made in

2026 has been a hard year for international travel — conflict and border tension across several regions, new tariff regimes, and measurably reduced cross-border leisure movement. Average order value across the business fell 10% over this period, driven by the boat line rather than anything in the checkout. Every gain above was made into that headwind, and none of it is adjusted for it.

The one place engagement ran ahead of spend: extras revenue per booking was level year on year, because travellers attach more and cheaper — damage cover at €43 a time nearly tripled in units. More people protecting their trip, at a lower average ticket. That is the brief for the next piece of work, and §9's instrumentation is what will measure it.

11 What I'd carry forward

Three things this project settled for me.

Research in one language is research with a blind spot.

The single most actionable finding in the study — that protection cards lack the numbers people need to commit — came from German participants only. Three of them, independently, hit the same wall. Had the study run in English alone, the highest-value, lowest-effort conversion fix in the whole redesign would not have been in the report. I'd now argue for multi-market qualitative research on any checkout serving multiple markets, and I'd argue for it on conversion grounds, not inclusion ones.

The frame is a growth lever, and it's free.

"Choose extras" to "Complete your trip". "Protection" to "Protect your trip". "Pay £1,169" to "Continue to review & pay". Not one of these changed a price, a product or a step — and together they change what the traveller thinks the page is for. Most of that reframing was done before I arrived, which is exactly why I'd defend it: I got to watch a set of pure copy decisions carry a structural result through testing. Framing is the cheapest lever in growth design and the one most often left on the table, because it doesn't look like work in a design review.

Design the measurement before you design the screen.

The reason this launch can be reported at all is that the instrumentation was specified before the first screen was drawn — which events, on which surfaces, with which denominators. The failure-state result stands up because an untouched error state sits alongside the designed ones as a control, and that only exists because someone catalogued all fifteen states instead of the six that were being changed.

Most design work cannot be evaluated after the fact because nobody decided in advance what evaluating it would mean. That decision costs an afternoon and it is the difference between a portfolio of screens and a record of what the screens did.

The cheapest growth work is the work you never repeat.

Experiment velocity gets treated as a process problem — more tests, faster cycles, better prioritisation. Most of the time it is an architecture problem wearing a process costume. A forked second brand, an accessibility audit that has to run twice, a payment rate hard-coded in four components, a metric nobody can compute without engineering time: each one puts a fixed toll on every future experiment, and the toll is paid forever. Removing a toll is worth more than winning a test, because it raises the return on every test after it. That work is invisible in a portfolio, and it is most of what separates a design that ships once from a product that keeps improving.

Building it is how the decisions survive.

The details that carry this redesign are not in any comp. They're the loading state that covers a three-second re-price, the token that inverts a confirmation colour between brands, the truncation rule that keeps a 400-character booking note from breaking a phone layout, the sold-out card that degrades without a dead button. Every one of those is a design decision, and every one of them only gets made if the person designing is the person building. That's the job I want: the one where the argument and the artefact are the same object.

The work was handed to the Le Boat engineering team for backend integration — Neptune pricing, Fiserv payment, session handling — with the measurement plan attached. The honest headline on this case study is not a number. It's that a structurally better checkout shipped with the instrumentation to prove or disprove itself, which is the only version of growth design worth defending.
