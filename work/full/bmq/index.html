00 What to expect

The short version, before the deep dive

This is a review of the Build My Quote page, done with data rather than opinion. It shows how people use the page today, what gets in their way, and eleven designs that respond to it. Five things it shows:

  1. 1

    The quote page is the busiest page on the site. 37,116 views in a week, more than twice checkout. Any gain here is worth more than on any other page. C3c

  2. 2

    People use it as a tool, not a form. A fifth of all clicks are paging the calendar. The pencil in the summary box is the top dead click. Three in four phone visitors never see the bottom half of the page. C6, C8, C20

  3. 3

    The number that matters is slipping. 23% of destination visitors reach Continue to Extras, down from 29%, while traffic grew 11%. Phones convert at 21%, desktop at 30%, and 58% of sessions are phones. L1a

  4. 4

    Eleven designs answer the findings, and all of them are here. They range from small fixes to the production form to a rebuilt flow for a phone. Three of them, variants 5, 9 and 11, are suggested as a first test against the live page; the reasoning is in section 09 and open to challenge. Before that, the team chose to user test two flows, variants 3 and 11; that part is in section 12. The designs · The user test

  5. 5

    Some things the data cannot yet tell us. The median leaver is gone in 1.6 seconds, before touching anything, so there are two kinds of visitor to read separately. The builder's own steps are not tracked yet, and two earlier tests have no read-out. T1

How to use this

Read it, interpret it, push back on it. The findings are what the data says; the designs are explorations, not a final answer; the test is a suggestion. Comments on any section are welcome, especially where your experience with customers says the data is missing something.

Read more below. Every section opens with a box called "the one thing"; that is the point of the section, and everything under it is the evidence. The longest evidence is folded so you can open it when you want it. The small tags are sources; section 13 lists them.

01 Summary

Every named pain point is one symptom

The one thing

Three complaints, one cause. The calendar, the long page and the pencils are all symptoms of a form built for a page that people use as a tool.

C6, C8, C20

The brief listed three problems with the quote builder: the calendar, oversized elements that force whole page scrolling, and the pencil icons in the summary box. The data confirms all three, quantifies them, and shows they share a cause. The page is built to be filled in once, top to bottom. People use it to compare options, many times over, and the interface fights them at every step.

Added 28 September: the analytics team's six month export and the eight past tests on the builder (section 10) corroborate the device split and the pencil finding, and add a second population: the median session that leaves does so within two seconds, before touching the builder at all.

The single most clicked element on the page is the calendar's Next button. The single largest source of dead clicks on desktop is the summary box's Passengers row, which promises editing and only scrolls. Half of desktop visitors and three quarters of mobile visitors never see the bottom half of the page. None of this is anger. Rage clicks are near zero. It is quiet, repeated friction on a page that gets more views than checkout.

02 Why this page

More views than checkout

The one thing

A point of conversion here is worth more than on any other page: 18.5% of all site sessions touch a quote page, and 58% of them are on a phone.

C2, C3

Build My Quote (BMQ) is the yacht charter configurator on every destination page of moorings.com. A visitor picks a charter type, party size, date, duration and yacht, and the page prices the trip and hands off to checkout. It is the commercial engine of the site, and the numbers say so.

In the seven days to 13 September the British Virgin Islands quote page was the most viewed page on the whole site, with more than twice the pageviews of checkout and almost three times its own destination page. Over 30 days, 18.5% of all site sessions touched a quote page. Any percentage point won here is worth more than on any other page.

The BVI page carries the traffic, with 4.5 times the entry sessions of the next destination. Every heatmap in this study is BVI, and the pattern was checked against Exumas before drawing conclusions.

03 The page today

Five panels, one order, one summary

The one thing

On a phone the builder is nine screens long, and the first choice, charter type, takes a third of it for a decision most people make once.

P

The builder stacks five panels in a fixed order: Charter Type, Number of Passengers, Choose Date, Your Duration, Available Yachts. On desktop a summary cart sits in a sticky right hand column. On a phone the cart sits at the very bottom of the flow, and a fixed bar carries the price and two buttons instead.

The Charter Type panel alone is four photo cards. The calendar is a six row month grid with a price in every open cell, 66 pixels tall per cell. Every yacht card is a photo, a spec strip, a price and a button. It is a lot of page.

04 Method

Numbers first, opinions second

The one thing

No opinion without a number. Every figure here is read from Clarity, GA4, six months of BigQuery, eight past tests, the live page or the theme code, and every one has a source tag.

The rule for this project was set on day one: every design decision traces to a data point, and every data point is stored word for word before anyone interprets it. The raw pulls live in a data file separate from this write up, each block with its filter, date range and a URL that reproduces the view.

Five sources were used. Microsoft Clarity for behaviour: dashboards, click, dead click, rage click and scroll heatmaps, and session recordings, all filtered to quote page URLs over the last 30 days. The squad's GA4 dashboard in Looker Studio for the funnel, device split, leads, revenue and error events. GA4 pageviews surfaced through Clarity's integration. The production WordPress theme, to confirm what each clicked element actually is and what it does. And live measurement of the page in a browser at 1440 and 375 pixels wide, to put heights against the scroll data.

Two checks kept the sample honest. Heatmaps were pulled for both desktop and mobile, and the BVI findings were re-run on the Exumas quote page, which has a fifth of the traffic and a different yacht list. The pattern held on both.

What this study cannot yet say

Which step inside the builder loses the most people. The GA4 funnel jumps from destination page view straight to Continue to Extras. The theme pushes an event for every step in between, but none of them is in the funnel yet, and Clarity has no funnel configured for the builder. The hypotheses are ranked by behaviour data and the overall drop, not by step drop off.

A note on Clarity's AI summaries

Clarity generates plain language summaries of heatmaps and of sets of recordings. They are quoted here as data, verbatim, because they are an independent reading of the same sessions. They are labelled as AI output wherever they appear, and every claim they make was checked against the ranked click data.

The three Clarity elements at the top of the dead click list are one thing in the user's eyes: the Passengers row of the summary box. The theme code confirms it. The row is a plain anchor to #passengers. The pencil is a CSS background image, not a control. There is no click handler. Clicking scrolls the page to the Passengers select. On desktop that select is usually already on screen, so nothing visibly happens.

Passengers is the field people most want to change in place because it is the parameter they re-test most. Session narratives show counts changed to 6, then 8, then back, mid session.

On mobile the picture is different. Dead taps are rarer, 2.7% of taps against 5.3% of desktop clicks, and they land on the charter type cards, not the summary: the "Skipper optional" ribbon, the Sail Fleet and Power Fleet titles and the card text together take 27% of mobile dead taps. People tap the photo and the name, not the button beneath. The summary box barely features on mobile because most mobile users never reach it.

5

People use the builder to compare, not to commit

Recordings show the same shape again and again. Change the passengers. Page the calendar. Switch sail to power. Leave to read a yacht page. Come back. Try again. Book later, or not at all.

Three sets of recordings were summarised and four individual sessions narrated: Clarity's top recordings for the builder, the 65 sessions with a dead click on the Passengers text, and the 6,520 sessions that clicked calendar Next. Every summary describes loops. None of the four narrated sessions booked.

The behaviour is not indecision about whether to sail. It is comparison shopping against a tool that resets context on every change, hides the options it already computed, and asks the user to hold the alternatives in their head.

Two things the brief did not ask about

The data surfaced a technical fault and a device imbalance that a design review alone would have missed.

Two JavaScript errors, one of them explained

Clarity reports "cartvalue is not defined" in desktop quote sessions. The string is not in the theme, the plugins or the page. It is in Google Tag Manager, tag 1793, a Microsoft Ads purchase event that passes cartValue as a bare variable instead of a tag variable. It throws on the order confirmation step, so Microsoft Ads is probably missing purchase conversions or their revenue. A tracking fault for analytics, not a fault on this page.

Two independent Clarity summaries also mention JavaScript errors on the BVI crewed quote page. That one is still unpinned. The theme guards the crewed path, and Clarity cannot isolate those sessions by the button text. It needs a reproduction with the console open before any experiment starts, because an unexplained error can skew an AB result.

C3b, C15, C17, C24 GTM container

Desktop is the harder experience

The conventional assumption is that mobile is where the friction lives. Here it is the reverse: desktop sessions show more dead clicks, more quick backs, more pages and longer active time. The sticky sidebar summary exists only on desktop, and it is exactly where those dead clicks land. Any redesign that treats the summary as a mobile afterthought has the priority backwards.

06 Diagnosis

A form being used as a tool

The one thing

The page is a form. People use it as a tool. Fix the mismatch, not the three symptoms one by one.

C17, C18

The page is designed as a form to fill in once, top to bottom. The data says people use it as an instrument to explore with, many times over. Every named pain point in the brief is a symptom of that mismatch.

The calendar is a search presented as a picker. It shows 31 cells of one month and asks the user to page until something looks right. The data to do better already exists client side: per date availability and the best price of the month are computed and thrown away.

The pencils are the interface's strongest promise of editing, attached to a jump link. On desktop the jump is invisible. Passengers, the parameter people re-test most, is the row they click most and the row that does least.

The length comes from giving every step the same visual weight regardless of how often it changes. Charter type is chosen once and gets four photo cards. Passengers is changed constantly and gets one select.

What must not change

The brief protects the page's logic, and so does this study. Same five inputs. Same pricing and availability rules. Same auto selection of the cheapest available date and yacht. Same URL sync and checkout hand off. Same GA4 events on the same selectors, so the funnel keeps working. What is open: order, size, grouping, disclosure and the summary box.

A constraint from earlier work

Availability is served through a caching layer, so an exact count can be stale by the time a user sees it. Any wording about how many boats or dates remain has to be banded, never a number. This rules out some obvious calendar treatments before they are drawn.

07 Hypotheses

Six things worth testing, in order

The one thing

Six bets, ranked by impact times confidence. The first three turn the ticket's three pain points into testable changes: show price across time, make the summary edit for real, collapse answered steps.

Each hypothesis names its evidence, the lever, and the metric it should move. None of them is a design yet. Order is expected impact times confidence from the behaviour data. The GA4 step funnel, when it arrives, decides the final test order.

H1 · Calendar

Show availability and price across time, not one month at a time

Evidence
Next is 16.7% of clicks, Prev 4.2%. 37% of sessions page months. Recordings show date loops.
Lever
Let people see more than 31 cells before committing: a price summary per month, a multi month strip, or defaulting to the best price month with an explanation. The per date data already exists client side. Counts must be banded, never exact.
Metric
Next plus Prev clicks per session down. Date changes per session down. Continue to Extras rate up.
H2 · Summary box

Make the pencil edit in place, or remove it

Evidence
41% of desktop dead clicks on one row. The pencil is a jump link.
Lever
Two variants, opposite directions, same hypothesis. Either the pencil opens an inline control for that field, or it goes and the row reads as a summary.
Metric
Dead clicks on the summary down. Passenger changes with fewer clicks. Continue rate up.
H3 · Page length

Collapse answered steps

Evidence
Charter Type is 1,722 px on a phone. Half of users never see the bottom half. Users revisit steps many times.
Lever
Once a step has a value it collapses to one line with its answer and reopens on tap. The page becomes as long as the open step, not the sum of all steps.
Metric
Reach of the yacht list and summary in scroll depth up. Time to first Continue click down.
H4 · Cards

Make the whole card the control

Evidence
Card ribbons, titles and text collect 27% of mobile dead taps. Yacht photos collect hundreds of desktop dead clicks.
Lever
The card, not only its button, selects the fleet. Same for yacht cards.
Metric
Dead taps on cards down. Charter type and yacht selection rate up.
H5 · Mobile bar

Give the bottom bar a way in, not only a way out

Evidence
58% of sessions are mobile. One in eleven reaches the full summary. The bar carries price and two buttons only.
Lever
The bar opens the summary, so the quote is one tap away from anywhere on the page.
Metric
Save Your Quote rate up on mobile. Summary interactions on mobile measurable at all.
H6 · Engineering

Fix the crewed quote error before any test runs

Evidence
JavaScript errors named in two independent summaries. "cartvalue is not defined" in desktop sessions.
Lever
Not design. But an unfixed error can mask or inflate any AB result on the page.
Metric
JavaScript error rate in quote sessions. Crewed Continue rate.
08 Test plan

One hypothesis per variant

The one thing

One hypothesis per variant. Measure Continue to Extras and Save Your Quote together, on the people who engaged. About 2,500 Continues a week carries two arms, not three.

L1a

The recommended primary metric is Continue to Extras per destination page user. It is the step the dashboard shows falling, it is already tracked as Add to cart, and at roughly 2,500 a week there is enough volume to detect a two to three point change in two to three weeks with two variants. Booking rate and revenue per booking are guardrails, so a variant cannot win by pushing cheaper trips. Saved quotes count as a secondary win, because the dashboard shows they book.

Each hypothesis becomes its own variant so AB Tasty can isolate the effect. Bundling the calendar change with the summary change would produce a number nobody could act on. Prototypes are built as explorations in the Helix design system, on the real tokens, so the winning direction moves to production without translation loss.

  1. Confirm the metric and get the GA4 step funnel from the analytics owner.
  2. Set up a Clarity funnel for the builder: page view, date change, yacht select, Continue.
  3. Engineering checks the crewed quote error and "cartvalue is not defined".
  4. Build H1 to H5 as separate explorations. Review with the product owner and commercial lead.
  5. Run the test whose hypothesis matches the biggest funnel drop first.

This was the plan on 15 September. The decision it led to, two arms against production, is in section 09.

09 Design progression

Two rounds, eleven variants, every one kept

The one thing

Eleven variants, every one kept. Three are suggested for a first test against the live page: variant 5, everything on one screen; variant 9, one question at a time; variant 11, which asks first and shows after. The reasoning is below, with why the other eight stay in the record.

E

The designs are Helix explorations on the real Moorings tokens, so what is shown here is what would ship, not a picture of it. They were built in two rounds and nothing has been deleted: a direction that loses in review is still evidence of what was tried and why the next thing was tried instead.

Round 1 patched the form. Four variants, one hypothesis each, everything else exactly as production. That is the right shape for an AB test, because a bundled change produces a number nobody can act on. It is the wrong shape for a redesign. Reviewing them side by side on 17 September, the pattern was obvious: every one of them fixed a symptom in place and left the page that produces the symptoms untouched.

Round 2 designed the tool. The brief has no design constraints, 58% of quote sessions are on a phone, and the diagnosis says people use this page to compare, not to fill in. The test we suggest

Eleven variants is a record, not a choice. The review on 28 September picked two arms against production, one for each of the two behaviours the recordings show, and the review on 1 October added a third for the visitor the first two assume away. Variant 5 is the compare arm, for the visitor who changes one thing at a time and watches the list re-price. Variant 9 is the guided arm, for the visitor who wants to be led and keep the quote in view. Variant 11 is the entry arm: it asks the four questions before it shows anything, for the visitor who arrives without a week in mind, which the six month data says is most of them. What is new

Engaged time before leaving and before Continue, six months
Share of quote sessions that clicked each control

How the engaged time finding fits the Clarity findings. They describe two populations on one page. Clarity says a third of BVI quote sessions page the calendar and a quarter have a dead click, with 4.6 minutes of active time per session; those are the visitors who use the page as a tool, and they are the ones the redesign is for. GA4 says the median leaver is gone in under two seconds; those visitors never engage with the builder at all, and no calendar or summary box change will reach them. Both are true, because the fast leavers set the median and the loopers set the click counts.

Two consequences. The diagnosis gains a hypothesis it did not have, H7 below: the first two seconds. And the test needs to read Continue on the engaged population separately (say, sessions with more than ten seconds of engaged time), or an effect on the loopers is washed out by the leavers.

3. Pre-selection distorts the calendar. The team's own reorder test (T3) names it: every calendar price is a seven night price because the duration defaults to seven nights, so a visitor who wants ten nights is reading the wrong numbers. Variants 8 and 9 already ask nights before the date; variant 9 prices the actual stay. This is the evidence for that order.

4. Save Your Quote is almost unused on a phone. Two taps in 35,252 mobile visitors during a 41 day test (T9). The sticky footer button is there and nobody uses it. For the metric decision this matters: on mobile, Save Your Quote can only move if the redesign gives it a way in (H5), and it will need a long run to be readable.

5. The tracking cannot yet measure the redesign. The Moorings events are clean (one productInteraction event per control, addToCart on Continue, share_quote with "Sent" for a saved quote) but they record which control was touched, not the value chosen, and carry no element id. A sheet open in variant 5, a step reached in variant 9, or a quote card opened from the phone bar has no event today. The report's own recommendation: add the selected value, a stable control id, and load timing and scroll depth on the quote page. That is a prerequisite for the test, not a nice to have.

6. Sunsail behaves differently. Charter type is clicked in 14% of Sunsail quote sessions against 43% on Moorings, the widest gap of any control. Either Sunsail defaults it sensibly or the control is hard to find. If Sunsail comes into scope, that is the first thing to check side by side; the Sunsail tracking also reuses one event for a page view and a filter change, so its numbers need the action filter applied.

H7, added 28 September

The first two seconds. Evidence: the median leaver engages for 1.6 seconds (T1); the quote page is the site's most viewed page and the tab strip puts it 537 to 721 pixels down (P). Lever: what the visitor sees before scrolling: a price, a count and the first choice above the fold, and load performance measured. Metric: Continue among sessions under ten seconds engaged; bounce. Variants 5 and 7 put results and price on the first screen; variant 9 puts from-prices on step one. Load performance is unmeasured and should be, before the test.

What this says about the arms

Nothing outside contradicts the two arms, and most of it is already inside them. Variant 5 is the marketplace model with the destination pre-filled: three chips, a sheet per chip, priced results behind. Variant 9's When step is Booking's flexible mode with a real calendar underneath: nights, then a month grid with from-prices, then a date. The calendar rules in variants 8 and 9 match the one pattern that labels its cheapest date rather than painting the grid.

Two things the patterns add that no variant has. First, flexibility as a named mode, "I'm flexible" or "Search without date", which is the industry's answer to the 975 users a week who reach zero yachts. Second, the product as a filter: no marketplace asks "which kind of boat" before it shows boats, and the team's own test showed that giving the charter step more weight lowered checkout entries.

What a better experience could convert to

Not a foolproof answer, a direction to hold the arms against. Keep what only The Moorings has, an instant price for a real quote, and borrow the marketplace's shape: one sheet asks when and how long (Dates or Flexible: nights chips, then months with from-prices, then a date), and who (a guests stepper); the yacht list arrives priced for that stay, sorted by price, with charter type and fleet as chips carrying from-prices; the pill at the top is the summary and the editor; the bottom bar carries the price and Continue. This is variant 5's shell with variant 9's When step and the charter step demoted to a filter. It was drawn the same day as variant 10 (section 09), and it is the merge of the two arms, and variant 11, built on it, is now the suggested third arm.

Patterns not to borrow

Google's date grid, a departure by return matrix: charters price by week, not by day pair, and it does not fit a phone. Kayak's three colour calendar: the treatment the sales desk already reports as confusing. Dream Yacht's lead form quote: it gives up the instant price that is the page's advantage.

The one thing

Before any A/B test, the team chose to watch real holiday bookers use two flows at opposite ends: today's page with answered steps folding away, and a pop-up that asks first and then shows the yachts. One question to answer: do people want the form, or a guided way in.

U1

Refinements made on the way

Walking the flows as a tester would found things worth fixing before strangers see them. Testing a flow does not mean the interaction can be clumsy.

  • Both flows. The page lands at the top, then glides down so Build My Quote sits at the top of the screen. The tab strip sticks once it reaches the top, as on the live page.
  • Flow A. After each answer the next step comes into view, and every step is walked in order, as production shows them.
  • Flow B. The pop-up opens in place, is wider so the steps rarely scroll, and keeps its button a human size. A button that cannot be used yet stays red, only faded. When the pop-up closes the yachts are on screen, not the photo, and the summary says any answer can still be changed.

The test plan and the team's review two lists

The test, as James set it out

  • Unmoderated, on UserTesting, as design exploration and comparison: good, bad and improvements.
  • Three pairings: Original against Flow A, Original against Flow B, Flow A against Flow B.
  • Eight people per pairing, desktop and phone run separately: 48 in all.
  • Screener: has booked a yachting holiday online before. United States, United Kingdom, Canada and Australia, aged 30 to 65 and over, household income $80K to $150K and over.
  • For each page: what it is for, how it feels, what confused, what they liked and disliked, one sentence. Then a preference between the two pages and one change they would make.
  • Results within 48 hours.

What Sophia added

  • Show the pages in both orders, four and four, and name the pages on the preference scale.
  • Give everyone the same task and ask them to stop before submitting.
  • Quick ratings after each page: ease, confidence in the quote, changing dates, editing earlier choices, knowing what comes next, page length on a phone.
  • A middle screener ready if the first fills slowly, and sailing experience recorded as a segment, not a filter.
  • Report themes and severity, not percentages, and agree up front what result moves us forward.
  • If budget gets tight, drop Original against Flow B first.
Still open
  • The live page fills in a date when it opens, while both flows start blank. The shared task, which makes everyone set March and four people, evens most of that out.
  • The LAB moderated research and its two visitor groups have not been seen yet.
  • The result that would take a flow to a production A/B test is not agreed yet.
