Everything happens in WhatsApp

At Project 831, my pickleball venue, people book a court, pay, get reminded, cancel, keep their money as credit, check their membership hours, renew a child's club and get the invoice, all in one WhatsApp chat. Nobody downloads an app. That was the first decision, made before any code, and everything else was designed around it.

Why WhatsApp, and not an app

In Jamshedpur, people message a business on WhatsApp. Before there was any software, every booking at the venue was a WhatsApp conversation with a person, and that was the problem: one person, one phone, every evening. The usual way out is to move customers somewhere else, to an app or a website checkout. An app asks for a download, an account and a password before anyone can play an hour of pickleball. So I chose the other way: keep people where they already are, and make the chat do the work the person on the phone used to do.

I'd spent a year and a half at BiteSpeed designing tools that help brands sell over WhatsApp, so I knew what the platform can carry: forms inside the chat (WhatsApp Flows), payments inside the chat, and approved templates for the messages a business starts. So the rule became: every job a customer has with the venue starts and ends in the chat. The website is there for people who find us on Google, and even it points back. After a web booking the bot writes, "By the way — you can do all of this right here on WhatsApp."

Customers and parents never install anything. The team does have a dashboard, but the rule held for them too. The coach was given the dashboard and never took it up, so the register takes his entries by chat as well. People don't change their habits for a tool. The tool has to come to the habit.

1. A court for Saturday, typed in Hinglish

Kunal doesn't open a menu. He types the way he'd text a friend. The bot spots "slot milega" and opens the booking form inside the chat. A weekend evening needs half paid upfront, so the payment card arrives in the same chat, and a reminder comes the evening before.

2. Price, paddles, directions: answered from what people type

Most messages to a venue are the same five questions. Priya asks two of them in one line. The bot answers each from its own wording, offers the next step, and knows when a conversation has ended.

3. Can't make it: cancel in the chat, keep the money as credit

Rohit paid a deposit for Sunday, then his plans changed. He cancels from the chat without calling anyone, and the ₹350 he paid doesn't vanish into a refund queue. It stays with the venue as credit and comes off his next booking by itself. If he'd rather have the money back, one tap asks for it.

4. Members: a balance of hours, spent in the chat

Ankit is in the 831 Players Club: prepaid court hours, cheaper than paying each time. His wallet is that balance. He asks how much is left in Hindi, and books against it with one tap. Joining happens at the desk, on purpose: nobody buys a pack of court hours from a chat bubble. After that, every hour is booked and counted in the chat.

5. A parent renews in two taps, and the invoice follows by itself

Neha's son Aarav is in the Off-Screen Club, the venue's weekend fitness club for children. Three days before his month ends, she gets a message with the fee and a button. She pays by UPI in the chat. Minutes later the invoice arrives as a PDF, and nobody at the venue has chased her or typed anything.

6. The owner hears about every booking, in Hindi

My father runs the desk, and he reads Hindi more easily than English. He doesn't open a dashboard to learn what came in. Every booking, cancellation, no-show and payment reaches his own WhatsApp, in Hindi, with the one number he needs: what is still to collect.

7. Even the desk works in the chat

When a parent hands me cash at the counter, I don't open a laptop. I type one line to the bot, and the register on the dashboard updates. Recording a payment never messages the parent on its own.

What WhatsApp's rules shaped for engineers

  • The 24-hour window. A business may write freely only within 24 hours of the customer's last message. Everything the venue starts, such as reminders, renewals and invoices, is a template approved by Meta: Utility for facts, Marketing for offers. Utility copy states facts and never persuades, or Meta files it as Marketing.
  • Two numbers. Parents and broadcasts go from a second number, so the booking number's quality rating is never put at risk.
  • Forms where typing fails. Dates, times and lengths are picked in a WhatsApp Flow. Free text is only for questions, and the matcher handles those.
  • Payments in the chat. Meta's order card, settled by Razorpay. A hold expires on its own, and the slot goes back on sale.
