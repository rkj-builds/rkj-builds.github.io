Price the stay, not the date

The most visited page on a yacht charter site is the one where you build a quote. It asks for five answers and a calendar is one of them. The calendar is where I lost two weeks, and it was the right place to lose them.

Sep–Oct 2026MineEleven variantsTwo flows in user testing

What the recordings showed

Three things, and all three pointed at the same place.

  • Next and Prev were a fifth of every click on the page. People were paging through months looking for a price the page would only show them one month at a time.
  • Two in five dead clicks landed on one row of the summary box. It had a pencil drawn on it and nothing behind the pencil.
  • Half of desktop visitors never reached the bottom half. On a phone it was worse, and most of the traffic is phones.

Eleven variants, one hypothesis each

The first four changed one thing each and left everything else as production, so a test could isolate the effect. The next seven reimagined the flow mobile first, each solving every finding by a different model: a quote bar, one question at a time, a price timeline, a search that refines, a guided entry sheet.

Five decisions inside the calendar

  • No Next and no Prev. They were the most clicked control on the page, which is not a sign that people love them. Months stack, and a strip of month tiles jumps to one. The tiles carry a from-price, so the choice is informed before the tap.
  • A price on every bookable date, in ordinary ink. Checked against Booking, which does the same, and Google Flights, which uses exactly one colour for cheapest.
  • One label, not a heat map. The lowest tier takes a single colour and the cheapest date in the month is labelled Best. A date that cannot be booked is struck through.
  • No red and green together. The sales desk reported customers reading the pair backwards, and it is the one pair colour-blind users cannot separate. Nothing on the grid is red.
  • The price is the stay, not the night. Seven nights from the 13th means all seven must be open, and the figure on the 13th is what those seven cost. Twenty-seven months of them, with the stay drawn across the grid and the end date marked Return.

The one I got wrong

In the guided variant the month tiles sit above the day grid. On a desktop that reads fine. On a phone, tapping a month changes something below the fold, so the page looks like it did nothing. I watched it happen and it reads as broken, not as subtle.

The next variant got its own picker: a month, then its days, in the same space, with a link back to the months. Same information, one surface at a time.

Where it stands

Two arms went forward for an A/B test later: the one that lets you compare, and the one that leads you. Before that, the team wanted to watch real bookers, so two flows at opposite ends are now in an unmoderated user test: today's page with answered steps folding away, and a pop-up that asks first and then shows the yachts.

One question: do people want the form, or a guided way in? Both prototypes are fully clickable, rebuilt to look like the live page, with no analytics and nothing submitted.

48 people on UserTesting, desktop and phone run separately, each comparing two pages: the live page against Flow A, the live page against Flow B, or Flow A against Flow B. Everyone gets the same task, 7 nights in March for 4 people. A clearly preferred flow goes on to a production A/B test.
